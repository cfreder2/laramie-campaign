Peer-to-peer tabletop

Open the table

One browser hosts the board. Every player connects directly by exchanging a pair of connection codes.

  1. Click Seat the four players — one code per character.
  2. Send each player their character's offer code.
  3. Paste each player's answer back into their seat.

Designed for players on the same local network. No board data is uploaded or stored by GitHub.

How to connect

No server required

  1. The host creates one player seat and copies its offer code.
  2. The player opens this page, chooses Join as player, and pastes that offer.
  3. The player creates an answer code and sends it back.
  4. The host pastes the answer into the matching seat. The board opens when the direct connection succeeds.

Two-finger trackpad swipes pan the map; pinch gestures and a traditional mouse wheel zoom. Left-drag empty ground to box-select actors; Space-drag, middle-drag, or right-drag also pans. Click empty ground or press Escape to deselect, or Cmd/Ctrl+A to select everything on the floor you are looking at. With actors selected, arrows or WASD move the group, Cmd/Ctrl+C and Cmd/Ctrl+V copy and paste it onto the floor showing, Delete removes it for the host, Q/E rotate a single selection, and C toggles its sheet from the right. Double-click or double-tap an actor to open the same sheet. Without a selection, arrow keys pan the map. With a sheet open, + increases HP and − decreases HP, and every other board key keeps working underneath it.

Floors, and the exits between them. Every screen shows the floor its own character is standing on — the priest is in the cellar and the party is upstairs, and both screens are right at once. A player changes floor by taking an exit: a link with two ends, each a square the encounter designer marked, carrying the words they wrote for it. Stand on one and Ways out offers those words; picking it moves your character to the other end and takes your screen with it. Squares that lead somewhere are marked faintly on the board. The DM's floor switcher is a camera and carries nobody — each button counts the tokens on that floor, and Bring the table to this floor is the one way to hold every screen on yours for a reveal, until you release it or a player takes an exit of their own. Ways out → Draw an exit makes one right here, live while dnd encounter serve is running: click the square it leaves from, switch floors, click where it comes out, then give each end its own words. Clicking an exit already on the board offers to remove it. This writes the place, so every encounter set there gets the stair — it goes through dnd place exit's own code, so the board cannot author one the CLI would refuse.

The sheet is the inspector. Double-click an actor, or press C with it selected, and its sheet opens on the right: its face, its statistics, what the table knows about it, and every control that writes to it. Health, armour, the damage threshold, the controller, rotation, size, inventory, notes, damage, and removal all live there, shown only to the DM or to the seat that controls the piece. A single click only selects — the sheet covers a good part of the map, so it opens when you ask for it and not before. Nothing about an actor is edited from the left-hand panel any more.

The bar and the rail. The title bar says which campaign and which scene, then one chip per seat — bone for a seat nobody has taken, amber for one waiting on its code, green for a player at the table. On the right sit View as player, Fit map, Save — live only while `dnd encounter serve` is running — and the menu carrying your name, Load encounter, Display settings, Players & connection, and this Help. The sidebar collapses to a column of icons rather than to nothing, so the floor switcher and the library stay one click away with the map at full width.

The host adds actors by dragging packs from the library onto a grid square. The library is shelves: non-player characters grouped by the outfit they ride for, creatures, then Weapons, Explosives, Gear, and Scenery. One search box covers all of it and matches names, affiliations, and a pack's own description of itself, so "ring" finds the Moyer Ring and the ring-gun both. The star on a card pins it to the top, on your screen only. Player characters are not in the library — The party lists every one in the campaign, seated or not, and the DM drags an unseated one onto a square. The Flame tool drops fire or smoke as a radius in feet (or paints it by dragging); each patch is one object you can wipe out with the Extinguish tool.

Affiliations are the DM's alone. On the host's screen every actor's outline is painted by its primary affiliation, so you can read the room at a glance: bone white means unplaced, a named outfit wears its own colour. The host manages the roster — rename, recolour from the western palette, add, or remove — in the Affiliations panel, and assigns an actor from its sheet. Players get none of it. No panel, no line on the hover, no chip on the sheet, and every outline on a player's board is bone white, so who rides with whom stays something the table has to find out in play. View as player shows you exactly that.

Names, health, and secrets. Open an actor's sheet and click its name to rename it — the new name reaches every screen. Hit points belong to the DM alone, except for the party, who always see their own; the DM toggles each bar on or off from the sheet. An actor is described by a list of facts rather than one paragraph: the DM types a fact and either tells the table, holds it back for a later reveal, or keeps it DM-only. Click the portrait for a full-size look at the actor.

Roll the Bones is available to everyone. A roll request goes to the host, which creates the authoritative result and broadcasts it so the same 3D dice animate and settle on the same values on every connected screen. The roller's name and result are retained in table history.

Network limitation: this serverless edition is intended for devices on the same Wi-Fi or wired network. Some browsers or restrictive networks may block a direct connection.
