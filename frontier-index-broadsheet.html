A Reckoning in Ten Days

The Commissioner Comes, and With Him the Whole of Our Fortune

Word is now confirmed upon the wire from Cheyenne that the Union Pacific's appointed Commissioner shall arrive in Laramie City within the span of ten days, bearing in his coat the determination that shall fix forever the seat of the great Repair Depot, the Round-House, and the Machine Works of the Western Division.

Let no citizen mistake the magnitude of the hour. A Round-House is no tent to be struck and carried off upon the next morning's freight. It is masonry and iron, the costliest structure the Territory has yet beheld, and the railroad does not raise such a monument save where it means to remain a hundred years. Whither the Depot falls, there falls permanence; and whence it is withheld, there the prairie shall in time reclaim what the surveyor's stake pretended to claim.

It is the plain estimation of this paper that Laramie City stands upon a knife's edge. Cheyenne covets that which we have laboured to deserve. What transpires in these ten days — whether order prevail or disorder; whether the stranger find here a city of laws or a den of pistols — shall be read in the Commissioner's countenance, and writ thereafter in the ledger of history.

All Citizens Summoned

A Meeting at Keystone Hall, This Evening at Sundown

Mr. Edward Ivinson, merchant and banker of Grand Avenue, has called the whole of the citizenry to assemble at Keystone Hall this very evening, that the town might counsel together upon its preparedness, upon the vexed question of certain land-titles, and upon the keeping of good order in the days now dwindling before us.

"What is decided in the next ten days," Mr. Ivinson informed this paper, "cannot afterward be undecided. I had sooner we decide it together in Keystone Hall than discover it decided for us by events."

Marshal Moyer and Division Superintendent Croft are both expected to address the assembly. The doors open at sundown. Let no household be unrepresented.

Notice of Deed Review.  The office of Justice of the Peace A. Moyer gives notice that three properties within the city survey-grid stand under formal review. Affected parties are urged to present documentation at the Justice's office without delay. The process is represented as routine.

Deputy Long Disperses
River Marauders

The Survey Line Kept Whole; No Godly Blood Spilt

The citizens of Laramie City are indebted this week to the prompt action of Deputy Steve Long, who, riding the eastern line in his office as territorial officer, came upon a party of Arapaho — some eight or twelve in number — unlawfully encamped within the survey corridor near Spring Creek, taking fish from the waters and obstructing the labours of the railroad.

When the contractor's foreman, Mr. Amos Holt, bid them disperse, the party is said to have refused and to have shown their weapons. Deputy Long thereupon intervened, and by his own account "spoke to them plain — that the railroad does not go around, it goes through, and the law goes the same."

The party withdrew without further mischief. "Steve Long is the reason this town sleeps of a night," said Marshal Moyer. This paper observes that the godless and obstinate elements which would deny godly civilization its appointed progress have found in Deputy Long a man not easily discouraged.

The Wages of Sorcery

A Conjuress Given to the Flame — The Mission of Saint Paylor Acts Where the Law Keeps Silent

It falls to this paper to record an event at once terrible and, by the lights of those who performed it, held to be necessary. On Thursday last the clergy of the Mission of Saint Paylor, under the hand of Father Dominic, did try, condemn, and deliver unto the fire the woman who called herself Madame Esmé — of no fixed origin, lately employed in the entertainments of a Front Street house — upon the charge of witchcraft and the practice of the forbidden arts.

The Territory, in keeping with the ordinances the Order upholds, suffers no practitioner of magic to dwell among the faithful; and where the civil law is yet too young and too occupied to act, the Mission has taken the office upon itself. So it was done.

Witnesses are not agreed upon what the woman did. Some say she made coin vanish and playing-cards obey her — the common tricks of any conjuror who works a saloon for silver. Others swear upon their souls that they saw a candle leap alight at her open palm, and heard a man's fortune told too nearly true to be the work of guessing. The distinction, Father Dominic holds, is one without a difference. "Whether the Adversary works through her power or through her cunning," he told this paper, "the door she opens is the same door. It must be shut — and shut where all may see it shut."

To that end the sentence was carried out in the open, and the stake, with its ashes, has been left standing before the church door, that no soul in Laramie City might mistake the gravity of the prohibition. This paper observed that not all who gathered watched to the end; some turned away, and a few were heard to weep. We render no judgment upon them, nor upon the Mission, but set the matter down as it occurred.

Let the lesson be taken as it is plainly meant: there is no welcome at the hearths of this city for those who traffic in powers that godly men are forbidden to hold. The wise will govern themselves accordingly.

The Banker's Corner

Conducted by Mr. Edward Ivinson, Grand Avenue

Let us speak plainly of money, which is the tongue in which the future is written. The Round-House, should it fall to us, is no mere building but a perpetual engine: four hundred wages, paid monthly and spent among us, each dollar turning thrice before it quits the Territory.

I do not say this to set men gambling. I say it to set men building. He who holds ground near the Depot site this day holds a deed of another character than he held last autumn. Plant your roots. A city that credits its own future gives the stranger cause to credit it also.

— E. I.

Of the Schoolhouse

Miss Hartley, Schoolmistress, Upon the Town's Conscience

Miss Evangeline Hartley, lately of Cincinnati and newly appointed to the instruction of our youth, spoke with this paper concerning the school-building fund of Mrs. Jane Ivinson, to which she has pledged her services.

"The question is never whether children may learn," said she. "It is whether the grown folk about them believe they deserve to. Laramie City is deciding what it believes this very week." The foundation is already laid upon a lot near the eastern blocks; doors are looked for within eight weeks.

Mrs. Ivinson, asked her purpose, answered only: "Laramie must be worth the living in. That is what we build."

Upon the Matter
of Vigilance

It is come to the notice of this paper that certain ranching interests of the county have undertaken what they style "citizen enforcement" — armed patrols, gatherings, and the keeping of lists of persons held undesirable. This paper does not impugn the intentions of such men as Mr. N. K. Boswell, whose devotion to order is not doubted.

Yet we would gently observe that Laramie City possesses lawful officers in the persons of Marshal Moyer and his deputies, and that with the Commissioner so near at hand, it is of the first importance that our city present to the stranger the face of Law — and not of the mob, nor of the rope.

Local & Particular

Lumber & Lots.  Green lumber arrives daily upon the line, and not a board sits idle. Carpenters report work enough for twice their number. A city is being built in the space of weeks; let us pray it is built to last and not merely to sell.

Of the Weather.  The spring runoff has the Laramie River running high and the Spring Creek crossings soft. Travelers to the Medicine Bow are advised to ford with care and to mind the bears, which are lately woken and ill-tempered.

Obituary

Mrs. Clara Boothe, of about one and fifty years, formerly of Ohio, was found deceased at her dwelling upon the survey-grid on the morning of the fourteenth of March last. The cause of Mrs. Boothe's death remains, at this writing, unknown; Marshal Con Moyer has assured this paper that his office conducts a full inquiry and that the matter shall be brought to the satisfaction of justice. Mrs. Boothe was known to her neighbours as a quiet and industrious woman who kept a fine garden. She leaves no family known within the Territory, and is interred without the city.

Mr. August Reimer, of eight and twenty years, of the railroad labouring crew, died of hurts taken in a fall at the Medicine Bow timber-works. The Company has tendered its condolences to his fellows.
